The idea

A little more room
for Klingon.

On a standard QWERTY keyboard, writing tlhIngan Hol can mean reaching for Shift to get the capital letters, or typing several letters to make one Klingon sound. On a phone, every key takes up precious space.

That's why I made quja'. The layout brings the letters used in Klingon together in a compact arrangement. The old page also described a word dictionary, so you could choose a suggestion instead of typing every word in full.

I sometimes forget what a prefix or suffix means, so the original version also had an optional list of them, using ASK's quick-text keys in place of the emoji shortcuts. These features need to be checked against the current package before they're presented as available.

What it does

It makes typing Klingon in the Latin alphabet easier. Capital letters and multi-letter sounds have dedicated keys, and the usual keyboard is still close by when you need it.

What it doesn't

quja' does not translate text. The pIqaD-inspired key images in the older package are decorative: tapping them still enters Latin-script text.

How to type

That's up to you. I found it comfortable to type one-handed: the keys form a pattern of three columns, one column, then three columns. Keeping your hand near the top row makes the keys easy to reach.

The actual layout

Four rows.
One compact pattern.

The preview mirrors the Android XML, including the wider delete key. The smaller pIqaD glyphs use the one-symbol-per-sound mapping used by the font, while the main labels show exactly what quja' enters.

That keeps the visual character of the older keyboard idea without pretending that the decorative lettering changes the text.

A note for phones

Keyboard, font
and Unicode.

A keyboard layout decides which text is entered. A font decides how that text looks on screen. They work together, but they are not the same thing.

In quja's Latin layout, keys enter ordinary Latin letters and letter sequences. For example, ch, gh, ng and tlh are typed as text; the keyboard does not turn them into pIqaD characters. A special font may change the appearance of letters in an app that uses that font, but it does not translate the text.

Unicode is the standard that lets devices and apps store and exchange text consistently. Installing a keyboard layout does not automatically install a font for every app on your phone. What you see depends on the characters entered, the fonts available to the app, and how that app displays them.

Why this arrangement?

Start from
the language.

Why not keep the keys as close to QWERTY as possible? Fair question. Counter-question: where would be the fun in that? jIqIDneH…

More seriously, I wanted a layout that felt natural on a handheld device with limited space. QWERTY was designed for English and for a mechanical device. tlhIngan Hol has its own sounds and rhythm, so I wanted to start from the language instead.

The first step was finding out which letters appeared most often. I counted letters in two sources: a word list from Klingonska Akademien and the restored Klingon translation of Hamlet. The figures were a rough guide for the design, not a scientific study.

The arrangement

Give the sounds
a place.

Order?

Once I had the numbers, I had to decide where to put each letter. Simply putting the most-used letters at the top seemed rather silly. I first tried putting the most-used keys in the middle, where they might be easier to reach with one hand. After a while, though, I realised that the layout needed more structure.

The shape of quja'

The letters tlh are among the least frequent in the count, but they have a prominent place: the word tlhIngan begins with them. The t key sits nearby. The other multi-letter sounds — ch, gh and ng — add a natural symmetry. And q and Q stay close together.

I also wanted the top line to read quja'. It may be a nonsense word, but it runs off the tongue as if it could be real.
